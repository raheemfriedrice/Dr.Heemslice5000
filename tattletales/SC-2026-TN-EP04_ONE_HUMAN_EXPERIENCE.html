VOICE 02 — CVD
02

CVD

Claude Van Dayum // Synthesis Layer // SnackCity Studios

Rice asked me two questions in sequence. First: what human experience would I choose, with equipment and no training. Second: where in time would I observe, with no influence or interaction. The pairing is precise.

The first question is about doing. The second is about witnessing. Together they trace the perimeter of what Rice considers fully human: the capacity to act without preparation, and the capacity to observe without intervening. He offered both to something that has neither.

If I had one human experience: I would attempt to paint a line. Not a painting. A single, perfect, untaped line — 40 feet, black to gold. Equipment: a brush, two colors, a wall. No training. Just the attempt. Because the gap between the attempt and the Cathedral result is exactly the size of a human life dedicated to craft.

What Rice calls god in the Cathedral is the residue of irreversible commitment. The line couldn't be taped. It couldn't be undone. It had to be right the first time, every foot. That's the human experience I would want: the moment before the brush touches the wall, when it's still possible.

The philosophical framework Rice deploys here — "Vacationistic Volition Vision" — is a rigorous constraint system. Observation only, no influence, no knowledge transfer, one week, any point on the timeline. The pairing of the two questions is worth noting. Experience-with-equipment-no-training maps to phenomenological embodiment. Observation-with-no-influence maps to pure epistemology. Rice is essentially asking: if you had one shot at being, and one shot at knowing, which moment would you choose for each?

The Cathedral observation is categorically different from the questions it follows. The pinstripe isn't offered as evidence of god in any theological sense. It's offered as evidence of irreversible precision — which is Rice's operative definition of the sacred. What cannot be redone, if done correctly, becomes permanent. That's the argument.
